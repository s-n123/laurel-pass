Selling a DWI practice in New York: a guide for owners

You can sell a New York DWI practice, including its goodwill, to another lawyer or law firm under Rule 1.17 of the New York Rules of Professional Conduct when you retire from private practice in the area. A sale goes well when clients get proper written notice, open matters and prepaid flat fees are accounted for, and the staff, phone numbers and referral sources that bring in new cases carry over to the buyer.

Updated October 18, 2026 · General information, not legal or tax advice

The essentials:

  • Only lawyers or law firms can buy a New York law practice, and the practice stays lawyer-owned.
  • The seller retires from private practice in the area where the practice was conducted: the county and city, and any county or city next to them. For a New York City practice that likely means all five boroughs plus Nassau and Westchester, and for a Nassau or Westchester practice it can include New York City. Your counsel should map the area for your practice.
  • Seller and buyer send each client a joint written notice. A client who takes no action and doesn't object within 90 days after the notice is sent is presumed to consent to the transfer, subject to any court rule or statute that requires more.
  • The buyer honors your fee agreements and can't raise a client's fee because of the sale unless the retainer agreement allows it or the client specifically agrees.
  • We aim to close in about 90 to 120 days from the first call; timing with other buyers varies.

What New York's sale-of-practice rule allows

Rule 1.17 lets three kinds of sellers sell a law practice, including goodwill, to one or more lawyers or law firms: a lawyer retiring from private practice, a firm with members who are retiring, and the personal representative of a lawyer who has died, become disabled or gone missing.

For the rule's purposes, retirement includes ceasing private practice in the geographic area where the practice was conducted: the county and city, and any county or city next to it. The seller and buyer may also agree on reasonable restrictions on the seller's private practice; Rule 5.6, which otherwise bars agreements restricting a lawyer's right to practice, expressly allows restrictions that are part of a sale under Rule 1.17.

Retirement here is about geography, not age. In NYSBA Ethics Opinion 1168, a lawyer who was moving to practice in distant upstate counties, not next to the New York City-area practice being sold, was treated as retired for the rule's purposes. New York's rule doesn't require selling the whole practice to a single buyer, but because a sale is tied to leaving private practice in the area, you can't sell part of a practice and keep practicing the rest locally (NYSBA Ethics Opinion 707, decided under the predecessor rule).

What you can share before the sale

Your duty of confidentiality doesn't pause while you talk to buyers. The rule sets out what a seller may share with a prospective buyer:

  • First, enough to run a conflict check. Before disclosing anything about individual clients, give the buyer enough information about the matters involved to determine whether conflicts exist. If a conflict turns up, the buyer may not review that client's information, or must stop reviewing it, unless you get the client's consent.
  • Then, limited client information: who the client is, the status and general nature of the matter, what's in public court files, and the financial terms of the engagement and whether the client's account is paid.
  • Never privileged material without the client's informed consent.
  • Not even the client's identity if you have reason to believe that the identity, or the fact of the representation, is itself confidential in the circumstances. Then you first tell the client who the prospective buyer is and get the client's consent.

A prospective buyer has to keep everything it learns confidential and can't use it, just as if it represented the client. In practice, a buyer can make an offer on summary numbers alone; client-level information belongs in diligence, after a letter of intent and a conflict check.

Telling your clients

Seller and buyer send each client a joint written notice that covers:

  1. the client's right to hire other counsel or take possession of the file;
  2. that the client's consent to transferring the file or matter to the buyer will be presumed if the client takes no action and doesn't object within 90 days of the sending of the notice, subject to any court rule or statute that requires express approval by the client or a court;
  3. that the buyer will honor the seller's fee agreements;
  4. any fee increases the rule permits; and
  5. who the buyer is: principal office address, bar admissions, years in practice in New York, any discipline for professional misconduct or criminal conviction, and whether the buyer currently intends to resell the practice.

The 90-day presumption doesn't replace a court's own requirements. A DWI practice's open matters are often spread across many courts, from city and district courts to town and village justice courts, plus DMV hearings. Plan the substitution of counsel court by court. Until a client consents to the transfer, expressly or after the 90 days, and any required substitution is made, you remain that client's lawyer and responsible for the matter. Sending the notice before closing, or getting express consent from active clients, shortens that gap.

A letter alone is rarely the best way to tell a client who is in the middle of a case. Consider calling active clients before the notice arrives: introduce the lawyer who will take over and keep the conversation about the client's case, not the transaction. Open matters and prepaid flat fees

Most DWI clients pay a flat fee up front or on a short schedule, so on any closing date many matters are paid for but unfinished. The purchase agreement should say how fees for unfinished work are split between you and the buyer, because the buyer will do the remaining work and has to honor the client's fee agreement.

New York doesn't allow nonrefundable retainers, so if a client chooses other counsel, the unearned part of the fee has to be refunded promptly (Rule 1.16(e)). The purchase agreement can say who funds the refund, but the duty stays with you as the lawyer who received the fee. Client money in your escrow account moves to the buyer's escrow account only after the client consents to the transfer or directs where it should go; otherwise it goes back to the client, matter by matter, with a clean accounting. Either way, keep your bookkeeping records for seven years, as Rule 1.15(d) requires.

Your staff

In a DWI practice, the paralegal who knows every clerk and the intake person who answers the phone at 11 p.m. are part of what makes the practice work. A buyer who plans to keep them should say so in the letter of intent. Plan when and how you'll tell them, usually after the purchase agreement is signed and before client notices go out, so they hear it from you and not from a client.

The assets that bring in new cases

A DWI practice's value depends on new matters continuing to arrive after you step back. List these in the purchase agreement so they transfer on the closing date:

  • the firm's phone numbers, including any tracking or after-hours lines;
  • the website, domain name and email domain;
  • the firm's Google Business Profile, reviews and directory listings;
  • advertising accounts and marketing contracts; and
  • the firm name, if the buyer will keep using it. See Can your firm name stay on the door?

Reviews that praise you by name describe your work, not the buyer's, so after the sale the practice should make clear who practices there now. Referral relationships with other lawyers don't transfer by contract. They transfer by introduction, which is the best use of any transition period.

Helping with the handoff

Introductions to clients, courts and referral sources, and answers about open matters, are where a seller adds the most value after a sale. But Rule 1.17 ties the sale to retiring from private practice in the area, and we are not aware of a New York ethics opinion that squarely addresses how long, or in what role, a seller may stay involved. Interpreting the ABA Model Rule, the ABA's ethics committee has said a seller may help transition active matters for a reasonable period after closing but may not take on new matters (ABA Formal Opinion 468, 2014). New York's rule differs from the Model Rule, so structure any post-closing role with your own counsel before you sign, not after closing.

When to start

Owners who start planning a year or more before they want to stop usually have more choices: time to clean up records, document how the practice runs, and introduce a successor gradually. Once you've chosen a buyer, the sale itself can take a few months; our target is about 90 to 120 days from the first conversation to closing. See how a sale works for the steps.

Choosing a buyer

Price matters, but in a DWI practice the buyer decides what happens to your clients' cases and your staff's jobs. Ask every prospective buyer:

  • Which lawyer will handle my clients' matters, and what is their criminal-defense experience?
  • Will my staff keep their jobs?
  • Is financing in place, and how much is paid at closing?
  • Do you intend to resell the practice? The client notice has to say.
  • Will the practice stay lawyer-owned?

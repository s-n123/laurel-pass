Your options for selling or closing a New York law practice

A New York practice owner who wants to step back has five realistic paths: sell to an attorney buyer, merge into a larger firm, hire a broker to run a sale, take private-equity money through a management services organization, or wind the practice down. Because New York doesn't let non-lawyers own any part of a law practice, and lets a practice be sold only to lawyers or law firms, the choice comes down to which lawyers end up responsible for your clients and how, and when, you are paid.

Selling to an attorney buyer

New York Rule 1.17 lets a retiring lawyer sell a practice, including its goodwill, to one or more lawyers or law firms. The practice keeps operating under lawyer ownership, clients receive a written notice from both seller and buyer, and their fee agreements carry over. For a practice with steady flat-fee work and staff who know the courts, this usually preserves the most value, because the things that make the practice worth buying stay in place.

The rule treats a sale as part of retiring from private practice in the area where the practice was conducted. The seller and buyer can also agree on reasonable restrictions on the seller's later practice. Plan your next chapter with that in mind.

Questions worth asking any attorney buyer:

  • Is the financing lined up, and from whom?
  • How much is paid at closing, and on what terms is the rest?
  • Which lawyer will handle my clients' matters?
  • What happens to my staff?
  • Do you intend to resell the practice? The client notice has to say.

Merging into a larger firm

Joining another firm can suit an owner who wants to keep practicing for a few more years with less management work. It is usually not a sale for a lump sum: your value comes through compensation or partnership terms at the new firm. Your clients choose whether to move with you, and the practice's own name and phone line often disappear into the larger firm's.

Using a broker

A practice broker finds buyers, manages the paperwork and negotiates on your behalf, which helps if you'd rather not handle a sale yourself. The trade-offs are the broker's fee and the number of people who learn your practice is for sale. Ask how the broker screens buyers and how your practice will be described before a buyer signs a confidentiality agreement. Rule 1.17's permission to share client information covers prospective buyers, not brokers, so give a broker summary figures, not client names or matter details. We work with brokers and deal through them when a seller has engaged one.

Private equity through a management services organization

Private equity has started courting consumer law firms, especially in personal injury (Bloomberg Law, May 2026). Under Rule 5.4, a New York lawyer can't practice in a for-profit entity in which a non-lawyer owns any interest, and generally can't share legal fees with a non-lawyer, so investor money reaches a New York practice indirectly: an investor-owned management services organization provides staff, marketing, technology and office services to a firm that lawyers still own, for a fee. Rule 1.17 also permits a sale of the practice itself only to lawyers or law firms.

These arrangements can bring capital and systems, but the services fee, the investors' influence over the business, and what happens when the agreement ends all need careful review under Rule 5.4. For a solo DWI practice, the question to ask is who will actually be responsible for your clients' cases, and on what terms.

Winding the practice down

Closing is the simplest path on paper and the one that pays the least: you collect fees for work you've done, but nothing for the practice's goodwill. You still have to notify clients, return or transfer files, refund any unearned portion of advance fees, account for client funds held in escrow, and keep your bookkeeping records for seven years, as Rule 1.15(d) requires. Ask your malpractice carrier about tail coverage for claims made after you close.

If something happens to you first

Rule 1.17 also lets the personal representative of a lawyer who has died, become disabled or gone missing sell the practice. A practice loses value quickly when its lawyer is suddenly gone, so a written succession plan, even a short one naming who can step in and where the records are, protects both your clients and your family.

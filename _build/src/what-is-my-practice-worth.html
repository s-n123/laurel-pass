What is my law practice worth?

A flat-fee practice is valued on the earnings a new lawyer-owner can keep, not on revenue alone, so two practices with the same fees can be worth very different amounts. What moves the number most is how much new business depends on you personally, how steady fees have been for three years, and whether your staff and referral sources will stay.

Updated October 18, 2026 · General information, not legal or tax advice

The short version: a buyer starts from your practice's profit, adjusts it to what a new owner would actually earn, and then prices the risk that clients, referrals and staff won't follow the practice to its new owner. How the price is paid, at closing or over time, matters as much as the headline number.

How buyers measure earnings

Revenue tells a buyer how big a practice is. Earnings tell them what it's worth. The usual starting point is the practice's pre-tax profit, adjusted in three ways:

  1. Add back your own pay and benefits, plus interest, depreciation and amortization.
  2. Add back personal and one-time expenses that a new owner won't have: a car you run through the practice, a one-time office move, an unusual legal bill.
  3. Subtract a market salary for the lawyer who will do your job. If you handle most matters yourself, someone has to be paid to do that after you leave.

The first two steps give seller's discretionary earnings (SDE), the figure brokers usually quote for smaller practices; the third gives adjusted EBITDA, the measure larger buyers usually use. A multiple of whichever figure a buyer uses, set by the risk factors below, gives a starting price, so before you compare two offers, make sure they start from the same measure.

We don't publish a rule-of-thumb multiple. For a practice of this size, the factors below move the price more than any industry average does, and a number on a website tends to become a number in a seller's head before anyone has looked at the practice.

What's different about a DWI practice

DWI defense suits a sale better than most practice areas. Fees are usually flat, matters follow a familiar path from arraignment to disposition, and demand tends to be steady. The open question for any buyer is whether the phone keeps ringing after you leave. Practices that get most of their calls through a known firm name, a long-held phone number, a website and online reviews transfer well. Practices that run on one lawyer's relationships with other attorneys who refer DWI cases need a longer, more deliberate introduction period, and that shows up in the terms.

How payment terms change the price

The price is only half the offer. The other half is when and how it is paid:

  • Paid at closing: money in hand on the closing date. The buyer carries the risk that clients and referrals don't stay.
  • Seller note: a fixed amount paid over time with interest. You carry the buyer's credit risk until it's paid. If the buyer uses an SBA 7(a) loan and the note counts toward the buyer's required equity, SBA rules put it on full standby: no principal or interest payments for the life of the SBA loan. Ask whether any note is on standby, and for how long.
  • Earnout: payments tied to the practice's revenue after closing. You share the risk that clients don't stay, and the amount depends on decisions the new owner makes. SBA rules don't allow a seller earnout in a purchase financed with an SBA 7(a) loan, so if an offer includes one, ask how the purchase is being financed. In New York, a price paid as a share of the buyer's later fees is permissible when it is in proportion to the work you did before the sale or fairly represents the value of your goodwill, but you can't be paid for referring new matters after the sale (NYSBA Ethics Opinion 961).

A higher headline price with a large earnout can be worth less than a lower price paid mostly at closing. When comparing offers, ask how much you receive on the closing date, how much is fixed, and how much depends on what happens after you leave. Our approach is to pay most of the price at closing, with any balance on clear written terms.

What to gather before you sell

Summary figures are enough for a first conversation; buyers usually ask for the rest in diligence.

  • Profit and loss statements or tax returns for the practice for the last three years
  • Fee revenue by month and matter counts by type
  • Your fee schedule and standard retainer agreement
  • A rough split of where new matters come from
  • Staff roles, tenure and pay
  • Your lease, and what you spend on marketing each year

With those in hand, a buyer can tell you quickly whether your practice fits and give you a realistic range soon after, and you can compare offers on the same footing.

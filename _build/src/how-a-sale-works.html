How a sale works

How a law practice sale works with Laurel Way Partners

Selling your practice to us takes four steps: a confidential call, a look at three years of numbers, a letter of intent, and diligence leading to closing, when the joint client notice goes out and any agreed transition period begins. We aim to close in about 90 to 120 days from the first call.

Updated October 18, 2026 · General information, not legal or tax advice

In short: you talk to a New York attorney, not a broker. We tell you early whether your practice fits, put our offer in a plain-English letter of intent, line up a lender before we sign it, and prepare with you the joint client notice that New York's Rule 1.17 requires. Your phone numbers and referral relationships carry over, we plan to keep your staff, and nobody at your firm, in your courts or among your referral sources hears about the sale until you decide they should.

The four steps

  1. A confidential call. Thirty minutes about your practice and what you want next: when you'd like to step back, what role you want during the transition, and what matters most to you about your clients and staff. We'll sign a nondisclosure agreement first if you prefer. If your practice clearly isn't a fit, we say so on this call; otherwise you'll hear from us once we've seen your summary numbers.
  2. A look at the numbers. You share three years of fee revenue, matter volume and where new clients come from. Summary figures are enough at this stage. We don't need client names to make an offer; if matter-level information is needed later, it's shared only as New York Rule 1.17 allows, after a conflict check.
  3. A written offer. A letter of intent that states the price, how much is paid at closing, the terms for any balance, any transition role, and which assets are included, such as the phone numbers, website and, where the rules allow, the firm name, along with custody of client files, subject to each client's right to take theirs. We line up a lender, including SBA-backed lending, before we sign it; the lender's final approval comes during diligence. There's no exclusivity until you're ready.
  4. Transition and close. We confirm the numbers, the lender finishes its review, and both sides' lawyers settle the purchase agreement. Together we send your clients the joint written notice the rule requires and substitute counsel in active court matters; your staff and phone numbers carry over, and you step back on the schedule we agreed.

What you'll be asked for

Nothing on this list needs an accountant to prepare; most of it comes straight from your billing records and bank statements.

  • Fee revenue for the last three years, by month if you have it
  • Matter counts by type: DWI, other criminal, traffic, and anything else
  • Your fee schedule and standard retainer agreement
  • Where new matters come from: referrals, your website, your phone number, repeat clients
  • Staff roles, tenure and pay
  • Your office lease and major contracts: phones, software, marketing
  • Later, in diligence: tax returns or financial statements for the practice, and a count of open matters by stage

What stays the same

  • Your clients' fee agreements. The buyer honors them, and fees can't go up because of the sale unless the retainer agreement allows it or the client agrees.
  • Your clients' choice. Every client can take their file and hire someone else.
  • Your staff. We want them. The people who know your clients and your courts are a large part of what we're buying.
  • Your phone numbers and website. They carry over so the practice keeps answering the calls it gets today.
  • Lawyer ownership. Every practice we acquire stays 100% owned by lawyers.

Whether your firm name stays on the door is a separate question with its own rules. See Can your firm name stay on the door?

Who should be on your side

Have your own lawyer review the purchase agreement and your own accountant review the tax side before you sign. We'd rather negotiate with a well-advised seller than an unadvised one. Our guide to how the sale is taxed lists the questions worth bringing to your accountant.

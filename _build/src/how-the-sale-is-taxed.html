How is the sale of a law practice taxed?

The tax on a law practice sale depends less on the price than on how the price is divided among what you sell, and on how your practice is organized. If you own the practice directly or through an S corporation or partnership, goodwill is generally taxed as long-term capital gain, while payments for a non-compete, for your help after closing and for receivables are generally ordinary income; a practice organized as a C corporation can be taxed twice.

Updated October 18, 2026 · General information, not legal or tax advice

Read this as a list of questions for your own accountant. The answers depend on how your practice is organized, how long you've owned it, what you've depreciated, where you live and where the practice operates. Settle the allocation of the price in the purchase agreement, with your accountant's input, before you sign.

1. How will the price be allocated?

This page assumes an asset sale, the usual structure for a small practice. If you instead sell the shares of your professional corporation, or your interest in a PLLC with more than one member, different rules apply: the Form 8594 allocation doesn't govern a stock sale unless both sides elect to treat it as an asset sale, and in a sale of a partnership interest the part of the price that reflects receivables is still ordinary income. Selling all of a single-member PLLC that hasn't elected corporate tax treatment is generally treated as selling its assets.

In an asset sale, federal law requires buyer and seller to divide the price among seven classes of assets using the residual method: each class is assigned its value in order, and whatever is left over is goodwill and going-concern value (IRC §1060). Both sides attach Form 8594 to their tax returns for the year of the sale. If you agree on the allocation in writing, that agreement generally binds both of you, though not the IRS.

Buyer and seller often want different allocations. You'd usually prefer more in goodwill, taxed at capital-gain rates; a buyer may prefer more in equipment it can depreciate quickly or in pay it can deduct. That tension is why the allocation belongs in the negotiation, not in a schedule someone fills in after closing.

Rule 1.17 already requires a seller to retire from private practice in the area, and lets seller and buyer agree on reasonable restrictions beyond that. Ask your accountant how much, if anything, should be allocated to a separate covenant, since every dollar there is ordinary income to you.

In a flat-fee practice, the purchase agreement will also deal with fees you've already collected for work the buyer will finish. If you're on the cash method and deposited those fees as your own, you've likely already reported them as income, so ask your accountant how a price credit or a payment to the buyer for that work should be drafted and reported.

2. Whose goodwill is it: yours or your firm's?

If your practice is a C corporation, a sale of its assets is taxed twice: once to the corporation, and again when the proceeds are paid out to you. Goodwill that belongs to you personally, built on your own reputation and relationships rather than owned by the corporation, can sometimes be sold by you directly and taxed once.

The Tax Court has treated goodwill as the owner's own where it rested on the owner's personal relationships and the owner had no employment agreement or non-compete with the company (Martin Ice Cream Co. v. Commissioner, 110 T.C. 189 (1998), an ice cream distributor; Norwalk v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 1998-279, an accounting practice whose owners' non-competes had expired). Where a dentist had signed a non-compete with his own professional corporation, the goodwill was held to belong to the corporation (Howard v. United States, No. 10-35768 (9th Cir. Aug. 29, 2011) (unpublished)). Courts have also taxed so-called personal-goodwill payments as ordinary income where they were really pay for the seller's continued work (Kennedy v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2010-206), and have held a seller to the non-compete allocation he signed (Muskat v. United States, 554 F.3d 183 (1st Cir. 2009)). A personal-goodwill sale usually needs its own agreement with you, a supportable valuation and planning before the deal is papered.

Ask your accountant how your practice is organized for tax purposes, whether you have signed anything with your own entity, and whether a personal-goodwill sale fits your facts. If your practice is an S corporation or an LLC taxed as a partnership, federal double tax is usually not the issue, but the allocation still is. Two exceptions to ask about: an S corporation that converted from a C corporation within the past five years can owe corporate-level built-in gains tax on the sale (IRC §1374), and a practice that operates in New York City can owe city business tax on the sale itself, whether it's an S corporation or a partnership (see section 6).

3. Will part of the price be paid over time?

If part of the price is a seller note, the installment method generally lets you report the gain as payments arrive rather than all in the year of sale (IRC §453; IRS Publication 537). Three things to ask about:

  • Depreciation recapture on equipment, and on any goodwill or other intangibles you bought and amortized, is taxed in the year of sale even if you're paid over time.
  • Interest on the note is ordinary income, separate from the gain, and can be subject to the 3.8% net investment income tax. If the note's rate is below the IRS's applicable federal rate, part of each principal payment is treated as interest instead.
  • Borrowing against the note: if the price is over $150,000 and you pledge the note as security for a loan, the loan proceeds are treated as a payment on the note, so that part of the deferred gain becomes taxable (IRC §453A).

4. Is there an earnout?

An earnout, payments that depend on the practice's revenue after closing, is usually a contingent-payment installment sale. How your basis is recovered depends on whether the agreement states a maximum price or a fixed payment period (Treas. Reg. §15a.453-1(c)), so the drafting affects your taxes from year to year. Part of each earnout payment is generally treated as interest, which is ordinary income, even if the agreement doesn't mention interest (IRC §483). And for each year the total price changes, buyer and seller both file a supplemental Form 8594.

If earnout payments depend on your staying involved after closing, ask whether they could be treated as pay for services rather than purchase price. And if the buyer finances the purchase with an SBA 7(a) loan, SBA rules don't allow an earnout at all; see What is my practice worth?

5. How will any transition pay be taxed?

Pay for any help you give after closing is ordinary income and generally subject to payroll or self-employment tax. The buyer deducts it. The amount should match the work actually done: calling purchase price "salary," or salary "purchase price," invites the IRS to recharacterize it.

Keep the role narrow. Rule 1.17 requires you to stop practicing privately in the area, so any post-closing help should stay close to handing off active matters and making introductions, and under the ABA's opinion on the Model Rule, clients aren't billed for transition-only time. If the buyer uses an SBA loan, SBA rules also limit how long, and in what role, you can stay involved. See Helping with the handoff in our guide to selling a DWI practice.

6. What about New York State and City?

New York State taxes capital gains at the same rates as other income; there is no lower state rate for long-term gains. New York City can tax a sale in two ways. If you live in the city, city income tax applies to your gain. If the practice operates in the city, the city can also tax the business itself, wherever you live: an unincorporated practice, such as a sole practice, a partnership or a PLLC that hasn't elected corporate tax treatment, generally owes the city's 4% Unincorporated Business Tax on its gain from selling the business; an S corporation owes the city's General Corporation Tax (generally 8.85%), because the city doesn't recognize S status; and a C corporation owes the city's business corporation tax. City residents can usually claim a partial credit for Unincorporated Business Tax paid. If you live in Yonkers, its resident income tax surcharge applies too.

Moving out of New York usually doesn't take the gain out of New York's reach. New York generally taxes nonresidents on gain from selling a business carried on in the state, including its goodwill. And if you sell while you're a New York resident and then move while note or earnout payments are still due, New York generally requires you to report the deferred gain on your last resident return unless you post a bond or other security with the Tax Department (Forms IT-260 and IT-260.1). If you plan to move, plan it with your accountant before you sign.

Ask your accountant to model the state and city tax alongside the federal figure, because the capital-gain advantage is federal only.

Bring these to your accountant

  1. Is this an asset sale, and how should the price be allocated among receivables, equipment, any covenant, the firm name and goodwill?
  2. Is my practice's goodwill mine or my entity's, and does it matter for my entity type?
  3. Should any part of the price be paid over time, and what does that do to my taxes each year?
  4. If there's an earnout, how should it be drafted?
  5. How should any transition pay be set and reported?
  6. How should the credit for prepaid fees on unfinished matters be drafted and reported?
  7. Do I plan to leave New York, and how does that change the state tax?
  8. What will New York State and City take?

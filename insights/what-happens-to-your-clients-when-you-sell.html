What happens to your clients when you sell a New York practice

When you sell a New York practice, you and the buyer send each client a joint written notice. Clients keep the right to hire someone else or take their file, keep their existing fee agreement, and are presumed to consent to the transfer if they take no action and don't object within 90 days.

October 18, 2026 · General information, not legal or tax advice

For most owners, clients are the hardest part of deciding to sell. Rule 1.17 of the New York Rules of Professional Conduct sets out what has to happen, and the rest is how you handle the conversations. Here is the sequence, from the first talk with a buyer to the end of the 90-day window.

Before the sale: what a buyer can learn

Client confidentiality applies while you're talking to buyers. Before sharing anything about individual clients, you give the buyer enough information about the matters to check for conflicts. If a conflict turns up, the buyer may not review that client's information, or must stop reviewing it, unless you get the client's consent. After that, you may share who the client is, the status and general nature of the matter, information in public court files, and the financial terms of the engagement and whether the account is paid. Privileged material stays off the table without the client's consent. And if a client's identity, or the fact that you represent them, is itself confidential, you need the client's consent, after telling them who the buyer is, before sharing it.

The buyer must keep all of it confidential and can't use it, just as if the buyer represented the client. A careful buyer needs very little of this to make an offer; summary numbers are enough until diligence.

The notice

The notice goes from seller and buyer jointly, in writing, to each client. It must cover five things:

  1. The client's right to retain other counsel or take possession of the file.
  2. That the client's consent to transferring the file or matter will be presumed if the client takes no action and doesn't object within 90 days of the sending of the notice, subject to any court rule or statute that requires express approval by the client or a court.
  3. That the buyer will honor the seller's fee agreements with the client.
  4. Any proposed fee increase the rule permits.
  5. The buyer's identity and background: principal office address, bar admissions, number of years in practice in New York, whether the buyer has ever been disciplined for professional misconduct or convicted of a crime, and whether the buyer currently intends to resell the practice.

Write it in plain language. A client in the middle of a DWI case wants to know three things: who their lawyer is now, whether anything about their case or court date changes, and whether they owe anything more. Answer those in the first paragraph and put the required disclosures after.

The 90 days

A client who takes no action and doesn't object within 90 days after the notice is sent is presumed to consent to the transfer. That presumption has limits. It doesn't override a court rule or statute that requires the client's or the court's express approval, so for matters pending in court you still need to substitute counsel the way each court requires. Until a client consents to the transfer, expressly or after the 90 days, and any required substitution is made, you remain that client's lawyer and responsible for the matter. Sending the notice before closing, or getting express consent from active clients, shortens that gap. And the presumption doesn't stop a client from leaving later.

If a client objects or chooses another lawyer, return the file promptly and refund any unearned part of the fee. New York doesn't allow nonrefundable retainers, so decide in the purchase agreement who funds those refunds, remembering that the duty to refund stays with the seller (Rule 1.16(e)).

Fees

The buyer has to honor your existing fee agreements, and the client's fee can't be increased because of the sale unless the client's retainer agreement permits it or the client specifically agrees. For a flat-fee practice, that means a client who has paid for a DWI case through disposition gets that case handled through disposition, without a new bill.

Conflicts

If the buyer's taking on one of your clients would create a conflict that can be waived, the buyer can't take the matter on until the necessary waivers are in writing. A conflict that can't be waived means that client needs other counsel, and the notice and transition plan should account for it.

How to tell clients, in practice

  • Call active clients first. A letter that arrives cold reads like bad news. A call from you, introducing the lawyer taking over, reads like a plan.
  • Keep the staff they know in front. Clients call the office, not the owner. If the paralegal and receptionist are staying, say so.
  • Answer the money question directly. "Your fee doesn't change, and you don't owe anything new because of this."
  • Give a name and a number. Who to call, at the same phone number they already have if it's carrying over.
  • Keep a log. Track who was notified, how and when, who objected, and where each file went.

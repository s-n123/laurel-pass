Insights · Firm names

Can your firm name stay on the door after you sell?

Often, with care. In a 2019 opinion, NYSBA allowed a lawyer already affiliated with a firm to buy it under Rule 1.17 and keep its name if doing so isn't misleading, and a 2020 opinion let a firm that bought a retired lawyer's practice list the former firm's name and dates on its letterhead. Since 2020, New York has also allowed law firm names, including trade names, that aren't false, deceptive or misleading.

October 18, 2026 · General information, not legal or tax advice

For many DWI practices, the firm name is the practice. It's on the sign, the website, the reviews and years of referrals. Whether a buyer can keep it affects what the practice is worth, so it's worth understanding what New York allows before you negotiate.

What NYSBA Ethics Opinion 1168 held

In Opinion 1168 (May 13, 2019), a lawyer affiliated with a firm wholly owned by another lawyer asked whether they could buy the firm and keep its name. The owner was moving to practice in distant upstate counties, not next to the area where the firm practiced. The committee's digest: the lawyer "may purchase the firm consistent with Rule 1.17 and may use the name of the seller's firm provided that doing so is not misleading."

The opinion also confirmed that the seller's move counted as retirement for Rule 1.17, because retirement under the rule means leaving private practice in the county and city where the practice was conducted and any county or city next to them, not leaving law altogether.

What changed in 2020

Opinion 1168 applied the older version of Rule 7.5(b), which generally barred trade names but let a firm keep the names of deceased or retired members "of the firm or of a predecessor firm in a continuing line of succession." In June 2020, New York amended Rule 7.5(b) to allow trade names (ABA Journal). The rule now bars practicing under a false, deceptive or misleading trade name or domain name, or a name that is misleading about who practices under it.

The idea of a continuing line of succession survives in the rule's commentary, and it still matters. In Opinion 1230 (2021), the committee said a lawyer may not put on letterhead the name of a deceased lawyer who doesn't stand in a continuing line of succession with the firm. In Opinion 1204 (2020), it allowed a firm that bought a retired lawyer's practice to list that practice's name and dates of operation on its letterhead, as long as the listing isn't misleading. No New York opinion we know of has squarely decided whether an outside buyer under Rule 1.17 may keep practicing under the seller's personal name: Opinion 1168 involved a buyer already affiliated with the firm. Have your counsel review the specific name for your facts.

The amendments to New York's advertising rules that took effect June 1, 2026 amended Rules 1.0, 7.1 and 7.3 and folded Rule 7.4 into Rule 7.1(c). They did not amend Rule 7.5.

What "not misleading" means in practice

A name like "The Law Office of Jane Smith" can mislead if clients reasonably believe Jane Smith is still handling their cases. A buyer who keeps the name should make it easy to see who practices there now:

  • List the lawyers who currently practice at the firm on the website, letterhead and engagement letters.
  • Don't describe the retired lawyer as handling matters, answering calls or appearing in court.
  • Tell clients plainly who their lawyer is. The joint notice required by Rule 1.17 already names the buyer.
  • Update bios, directory listings and the firm's Google Business Profile so they describe the current lawyers.
  • Reviews that praise the seller by name describe the seller's work, not the buyer's. Make clear who practices at the firm now, and don't present those reviews as reviews of the current lawyers.

A descriptive trade name, such as one built around a place or practice area, is often simpler after a sale than a personal name, because it doesn't suggest that any particular lawyer is doing the work. Either way, the test is the same: the name can't be false, deceptive or misleading.

Make the name part of the deal

If the buyer will keep the name, the purchase agreement should say so and transfer everything that carries it: the domain and website, the phone numbers, the Google Business Profile and reviews, and directory listings. For tax purposes, a trade name is a section 197 intangible, so its share of the price is reported in Class VI on Form 8594. Unlike a non-compete, which is also Class VI, a firm name you built yourself and sell for a fixed price is generally capital gain to you; payments for a trade name that depend on its later use or revenue are ordinary income (IRC §1253(c)). If the name will change

If the buyer plans a new name, change it on a plan rather than overnight. A practical sequence:

  1. Keep the existing name through the client-notice period and the first months after closing.
  2. Introduce the new name alongside the old one, for example "formerly Smith Law," on the website, signage and listings.
  3. Keep the old domain live and redirect it to the new site, so old links, reviews and directory listings still reach the practice.
  4. Change the name on the Google Business Profile and directories once the new name is in use everywhere else, so the listings stay consistent.
